The Ethics of Manipulative Web Design

By: Bryce Burns

When you're browsing online, have you ever felt nudged or almost feel like you're forced into a subscription with hidden cost or the cost of your personal information? Well you’re not alone, it's the result of dark patterns in the marketing world and not just a clever market. These companies use deliberate design choices and their goal is to manipulate you into making an action that you may have never taken. In this article you will explore the different tactics and what they are, why they are used, and whether they are morally defensible.

What are Dark Patterns?

Dark patterns are user interface (UI) designs that are designed to trick or influence users into a decision that benefits the company, even if it is at your expense.

Common dark patterns in design:
  • Drop Pricing: Showing a low upfront cost, then adding hidden fees at a later point.
  • Confirmshaming: Guilt tripping the user into opting in (e.g., “Are you sure you don’t want to stay informed?”).
  • Roach Motel: A process in which it is easy to sign up but difficult to cancel.
  • Misdirection: Drawing your attention to one particular option that is more profitable while hiding less valuable options.
  • Forced Continuity: Automatically renewing a subscription without clear reminders.
  • Privacy Zuckering: Manipulating you into sharing more personal information than you normally would.

So when you are browsing keep in mind these aren’t mistakes in the design, they are specific choices using intentional tactics to influence you.

How Prevalent Are These Manipulative Designs?

You may think that these dark patterns are rare, but research shows they are extremely common:

  • In one large study, researchers identified 1,818 dark pattern instances across 11,000 shopping websites.
  • In a 2024 international sweep by the Global Privacy Enforcement Network (GPEN), 97% of the websites tested used at least one dark pattern.
  • In mobile apps, a 2020 study revealed that 95% of free Android apps within the U.S. had dark patterns within their app.

Seeing how widespread this problem is, it’s not just a small issue with certain websites—almost every website is built with dark patterns front and center.

Why Do Websites Use Manipulative Designs?

From a business perspective, there are strong incentives to use these deceptive tactics:

  • Conversion & Revenue: These tactics can boost sales, sign-ups, or data collection more aggressively than casual approaches.
  • Behavioral Leverage: Designers apply insights from behavioral economics, exploiting natural instincts to influence decisions.
  • Lack of Regulation: Many deceptive tactics exist in legal grey areas, with enforcement still catching up.

However, there is also a long-term risk: lost trust, customer backlash, and potential legal repercussions.

The Ethical Concerns: Is It “Just Business,” Or Is It Wrong?
  • Freedom to choose vs. being tricked: Dark patterns undermine your ability to make informed, voluntary decisions.
  • Trust & Long-Term Relationships: While they can boost short-term profits, they damage user trust and loyalty.
  • Regulation & Accountability: Growing concern suggests some dark patterns may violate deceptive trade statutes.
When Is It Morally Acceptable To Use Manipulative Design?

The article weighs situations like free trials, data consent, and engagement nudges, arguing that:

  • If design relies on deception, confusion, or coercion, it is likely morally questionable.
  • Design that is transparent, clear, and respectful of the user supports well-being and long-term trust.
What Can Designers and Businesses Do Instead?
  • Prioritize Transparency: Make costs, options, and consequences clear.
  • Offer Genuine Choice: Respect user decisions without shaming.
  • Simplify Cancellation: Make it just as easy to leave as it was to join.
Conclusion

You deserve an online experience that respects your freedom to make your own choices, not one that pressures you into something you never intended. While manipulative design may seem like standard business practice, it raises serious concerns about trust, fairness, and control online. As a designer, business owner, or user, the real question is whether short-term gains are worth sacrificing honesty and respect.

When is it right to eat meat?

By: Bryce Burns – Comp II Research Paper

Every year, humans raise and kill billions of animals for food, but how many of them live without suffering endlessly in their life? The moral question of whether it is right or wrong to eat meat may be the wrong question to answer. Instead, we should ask how these animals are treated before they reach our plate. For decades vegetarians have debated meat eaters on why they think it's wrong to eat meat. They argue that it’s harmful to the environment (Brooks) and contributes to climate change. Others believe humans have the basic right to eat meat. I fall somewhere in the middle of this moral battle.

Since Bible times, humans have consumed meat and this should not be stopped. I believe that while eating meat isn’t necessarily immoral, it’s only ethically justifiable if animals are raised and treated humanely, minimizing as much suffering as we can. To support this, I argue that animals can be treated humanely, that we have a moral obligation to minimize suffering, and that practical solutions exist to ensure we can consume ethically harvested meat. I also address counterarguments such as “all meat consumption is immoral” and “factory farming dominates the meat industry.”

Humane Treatment and Animal Welfare

Eating meat from animals can be morally acceptable when those animals are raised correctly and not confined in cages with severely limited movement. In the study titled The Benefits of Improving Animal Welfare from the Perspective of Livestock Stakeholders across Asia by Michelle Sinclair, Claire Fryer, and Clive J. C. Phillips, researchers explore the benefits of improving animal welfare across major Asian livestock producers. They find that financial gains through increased productivity and improved meat quality are major incentives for companies to enhance animal welfare. This supports the idea that when animals are treated fairly, consuming meat from them is more ethically defensible.

Moral Obligation to Minimize Suffering

In our time, humans have a moral responsibility to help minimize suffering, even in animal farms. Peter Singer, a well-known ethicist, argues in Animal Liberation that reducing suffering is central to basic human morals. He believes it's not intelligence or species that matters, but the ability to suffer and feel pain. This suggests it is morally wrong to eat meat if we cause unnecessary suffering and fail to treat animals with care. His ideas support the claim that while it’s not inherently wrong to consume meat, we must ensure animals are properly cared for while alive and protected by laws that hold farms accountable.

Counterarguments: Is All Meat Consumption Immoral?

Some people, such as vegans and vegetarians, disagree and argue that all meat consumption is immoral because it involves taking a life. In Jonathan Safran Foer’s book Eating Animals, he discusses how animals can suffer but suggests the real question is whether we can justify eating them. Foer himself struggles to completely give up meat, describing a diet that is plant-based during the day and vegetarian at night. This aligns with my view that the focus should be on how animals are treated and whether their lives are valued rather than dismissed.

When we eat meat produced by large industrial plants that disregard animal welfare, we indirectly support ongoing animal abuse. This makes it crucial for consumers to think critically about where their meat comes from and what kinds of practices they are supporting.

Practical Solutions: Ethical Farming and Certifications

The idea of eating meat ethically isn’t far-fetched. Anyone who wants to switch to ethically raised meat has options. Organizations like Certified Humane and Animal Welfare Approved provide certifications that monitor farms and ensure humane practices. Their standards require adequate space, proper nutrition, and access to outdoor areas, leading to higher animal welfare and better-quality meat.

In Arkansas, some Certified Humane farms are located in Crawford and Stone counties, showing that ethical options are available locally. When humans choose ethically raised meat, they support better treatment of animals and smaller farms that prioritize welfare. These certifications help consumers identify meat that aligns with their values.

Critiques: Factory Farming and Industry Dominance

Critics argue that factory farming dominates the industry, making it extremely difficult to ensure animals are treated with respect. These farms are often overcrowded, confining animals with limited access to outdoors and leading to significant suffering. Documentaries like Food, Inc. and Cowspiracy provide insight into these conditions, highlighting the gap between the ideal of “family farms” and the reality of industrial agriculture.

While factory farming is a serious concern, it does not mean that all meat consumption is immoral. Instead, it challenges consumers to make informed, ethical choices. By seeking out certified humane farms and supporting higher welfare standards, people can align their morals with their diets and push the market toward better practices.

Conclusion

To summarize, eating ethical meat is both possible and important. This shifts the debate from a simple “right or wrong” stance to a more nuanced view focused on how animals are treated. Studies like Sinclair’s show that improved welfare can benefit both animals and producers. Ethicists like Peter Singer highlight our responsibility to minimize suffering, reinforcing the need to care for animals throughout their lives.

While some argue that all meat consumption is immoral, these views often overlook progress in ethical farming. Certifications like Certified Humane help consumers identify meat produced under higher welfare standards. Critics are right to call out factory farming, but refusing all meat is not the only solution. The more important question is not whether we should eat meat, but how we can do so in a way that aligns with our moral beliefs. Together, as a society, we can move toward a future where eating meat and humane treatment of animals can coexist.
